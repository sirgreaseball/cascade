1. What it is

Cascade simulates what happens downstream when a dam breaches, a landslide or glacial lake bursts, a spillway opens, or a cloudburst lands on a whole basin: how much water leaves, where it goes, when it arrives, how deep and fast it gets, who and what is in the way — and it checks itself against satellite observation and analytical benchmarks.

Everything runs client-side in the browser. No backend, no server cost, no API keys. That is a deliberate design decision: it means the tool runs on a disconnected laptop in a district office during an emergency.

Four event types are modelled: dam break, lake outburst (moraine or landslide dam), controlled release (gates opened on purpose), and cloudburst (rain on every cell, nothing fails).

Two architectural rules we enforce strictly

  1. No Transferable buffers across the worker boundary. Everything crosses by structured cloning. Transferring detaches the buffer from the sender; in an earlier version that made the water render invisible because React and deck.gl still held a detached array.
  2. The worker and the main-thread fallback drive the solvers through one identical call path (createRuntime), so arguments can never silently drift apart. New solver parameters go into EngineConfig, never into a second hand-maintained argument list. The cloudburst was added exactly this way: one optional storm field on EngineConfig, read by both the CPU and the GPU solver.

4. The physics — how water flow is computed

The equations

The 2D shallow-water equations (depth-averaged Navier–Stokes) — the same physics Delft3D-FLOW uses in 2D mode:

∂h/∂t + ∇·(h·u) = R − I                                  (mass, with rain R and losses I)
∂(h·u)/∂t + ∇·(h·u⊗u) + g·h·∇(h + z) = −g·n²·|u|·u / h^⅓   (momentum)

h = water depth, u = depth-averaged velocity, z = bed elevation, n = Manning roughness, g = 9.81 m/s², R = rainfall rate, I = infiltration. For a dam break R and I are zero and all the water enters at the breach; for a cloudburst they are the whole story.

The numerical method

  • Godunov finite volumes with HLL Riemann fluxes (Toro, 2001). At every cell face we solve a Riemann problem — the exact “what happens when two states meet”. This is what lets the scheme capture bores and hydraulic jumps as sharp discontinuities instead of smearing them.
  • Hydrostatic reconstruction (Audusse et al., 2004) makes the scheme well-balanced: still water over uneven ground stays perfectly still, and depths stay positive at wet/dry fronts. Many codes fail this and generate phantom currents.
  • Explicit time stepping at CFL 0.45, capped at 10 s. The step is recomputed every iteration from the fastest wave speed present, (|u| + √(g·h)) / Δx, so water never crosses more than a fraction of a cell per step.
  • Point-implicit Manning friction: applied as a damping factor 1 + Δt·g·n²·|u| / (h·∛h), which stays stable in very shallow water where an explicit treatment would blow up.
  • Open outflow boundaries at the domain edge, with the volume leaving tracked so mass balance always closes.
  • Speed capped at 45 m/s; momentum zeroed below 1 mm depth — numerical hygiene for thin films.

The speed optimisation

The solver tracks per-row wet spans with a two-cell halo, iterating only cells that hold water or could receive it next step, instead of sweeping the whole grid — roughly a 1.8× speedup. A cloudburst deliberately defeats this: while it is raining every cell holds water, so the spans are forced to the full grid and the solver does the whole domain. That is not a bug, it is what rain on a basin means.

5. The breach model — where the water comes from

A dam break is not “remove the dam”. We model:

  • Breach geometry and timing from Froehlich (2008): average width B = 0.27·k₀·V^0.32·h_b^0.04, formation time t_f = 63.2·√(V / (g·h_b²)), with side slopes by failure mode (overtopping vs piping).
  • Level-pool reservoir routing: the reservoir drains through the growing breach as a weir, and crucially against the live tailwater — water already downstream pushes back and submerges the breach, reducing outflow.
  • For comparison the app shows two published regressions: Froehlich (1995) peak 0.607·V^0.295·H^1.24, and MacDonald & Langridge-Monopolis (1984) 1.154·(V·H)^0.412.

This is why the interface reports a modelled, tailwater-coupled peak first, and labels the free-outflow figure as a bound rather than a prediction.

River blockages measure their own lake

A landslide or debris dam is not a surveyed reservoir — nobody knows its volume. src/lib/blockageLake.ts therefore does not ask for one: it draws the barrier as a line of blocked cells across the flow, fills the valley upstream to the barrier crest by a level-pool flood fill on the DEM, and reports the impounded volume, surface area, deepest water and whether the lake reaches the edge of the study area (in which case the volume is a lower bound). The scenario builder shows the measured figure — for example “blockage lake measured from the terrain: 12.4 Mm³ over 0.83 km²”.

6. Cloudburst — rain on the grid

The problem statement asks for flash floods as well as dam breaks. A flash flood has no reservoir and no breach: rain falls on the whole basin and runs downhill. Cascade models it as a source term on every cell rather than an inflow at a point.

What the user sets

ControlMeaningDefault
RainfallRate over the whole basin while the storm lasts100 mm/h
Storm lastsDuration from t = 01 h
Ground takesInfiltration plus interception, removed from standing water at all times8 mm/h

India’s meteorological definition of a cloudburst is 100 mm in an hour, which is the default. Kedarnath in June 2013 took roughly 325 mm in a day. Saturated Himalayan slopes in the monsoon shed almost everything, which is why the losses default to single figures.

How it is solved

  • EngineConfig.storm = { intensity, duration, infiltration }, all in metres per second, built by setupSimulation from the mm/h the user sets.
  • CPU solver: before the flux sweep each step, h += (rain − infiltration)·Δt over the whole grid, clamped at zero, with gross rain counted into the inflow volume so the water balance still closes. While it is raining the wet spans are forced to the full domain.
  • GPU solver: the same term inside the update kernel, gated on P.t0 + state[0] < P.stormEnd, with the rain volume accumulated in prepare. Every tile is woken at start-up, since rain falls everywhere.
  • SPH solver cannot be rained on — it has no cells — so it receives the same storm as a lumped runoff hydrograph: net rain over the contributing catchment passed through a single linear reservoir with a 30-minute lag, entering at the head of the reach. The grid answer is the physically better one; SPH is the independent check.
  • Because the rain is already falling on the grid solver’s domain, its point inflow is reduced to base flow only — otherwise the storm would be counted twice.

What it produces

Measured headless on Rishi Ganga (100 mm/h for 1 h, 8 mm/h losses, 2 h simulated): the wet area climbs to 58 km² during the storm, then drains back to 17 km² as the sheet infiltrates and the runoff concentrates into the channels — which is exactly the signature of a flash flood rather than a uniform pond. In the browser, a Tehri-basin cloudburst reaches 289 settlements and 51.7K people, first water at T+10:36, 54 bridges under water.

7. The second solver — SPH

SPH-SWE: smoothed-particle hydrodynamics in shallow-water form with variable smoothing length, after Vacondio et al. (2012). The flood is carried by 3,000 / 6,000 / 12,000 moving parcels of water instead of fixed cells, integrated with symplectic Euler and the same implicit Manning friction.

Why two solvers: they fail in different ways. SPH has no fixed grid, so its wet/dry front is not smeared across a cell. Where the two agree, the result does not depend on the choice of method; where they differ, the gap is a measure of model uncertainty. The Difference layer maps that gap.

Read the difference honestly. SPH consistently spreads wider than the grid solver — on Rishi Ganga it floods 4.6 km² against the grid’s 2.1 km², an extent ratio near 2×. An earlier arrival in SPH is therefore not evidence that the grid solver is late; it is the known bias of a particle method with far fewer degrees of freedom. The right reading is “the two methods bracket the answer”, and the interface reports the critical success index between them rather than picking a winner.

8. The GPU engine, and the race

The grid solver can run on the graphics card through WebGPU, as four WGSL compute kernels over a 16 × 16 tiled domain:

KernelJob
prepareChoose the time step from the live CFL rate, inject breach inflow, accumulate rain volume (one thread)
updateFour face fluxes per cell, update depth and momentum, add rain and subtract infiltration, apply friction, update the peak-depth / arrival / velocity envelopes (one thread per cell, tiles that are asleep return immediately)
reducePer tile: the largest wave speed, the outflow through the edge, and whether the tile holds water
finalizeCombine tile partials into the global CFL rate, accumulate outflow, advance the clock, and wake the neighbours of every wet tile

State lives in ping-pong vec4 buffers (depth, x-discharge, y-discharge, bed elevation). The reservoir stays on the CPU and couples in batches of ≤30 simulated seconds: the host writes the inflow at the start and end of a batch, the GPU interpolates it in time and reports the volume it added, which then leaves the reservoir. Submissions are sized to about 8 ms of GPU work so the map keeps drawing between them.

The race — and why the processor usually wins.

A browser hands WebGPU whichever adapter it likes, and on a laptop that is almost always the integrated chip — the same one drawing the map. This solver’s per-step cost is mostly fixed (four dispatches, two of them single-threaded), so on integrated graphics it is dominated by overhead rather than arithmetic.

So Cascade no longer assumes. At the start of a run both solvers are given the same opening 30 simulated seconds and timed; the graphics card is kept only if it is within 2× of the processor, with that slack because the early flood is at its smallest and the GPU scales better as the water spreads. The verdict is cached per grid size per session and reported in Model → This device.

On the development laptop the verdict reads: “the graphics card the browser gave it (gen-12lp) solved the first half-minute 86× slower than the processor.” Two hours of Tehri, measured end to end in the browser: 171–180 s on that integrated GPU versus 6 s in the Web Worker. Compute rate: 0.7 against 10–20 simulated minutes per second of wall clock.

The switch in the Model tab is therefore labelled “Let the grid solver try the graphics card” — it grants permission, it does not force the issue. On a machine with a real discrete GPU the race is won by the card and it is used.

On Malpasset

Malpasset (France, 1959) is the classic real-world validation case, but its benchmark topography is a digitised 1931 survey map — used precisely because the Reyran valley was reshaped beyond recognition by the flood and later development. Modern SRTM terrain is the wrong landscape, so a Malpasset run on it would be meaningless however convincing it looked, and the CADAM dataset with the survey-point coordinates is not openly redistributable. We use the reproducible Environment Agency case instead, and say so.

11. Bugs we found — and what they demonstrate

Mass appearing from nowhere — a real defect in our own solver

Building the Environment Agency benchmark exposed a mass-conservation failure: a sealed basin finished with 10,584 m³ after only 771 m³ was released (+1362 %).

Cause. The time step is chosen from the fastest water present — but at the start the valley is dry, so there is nothing to measure and it fell back to its 10 s ceiling. A safety check existed for incoming water, but it sampled the inflow at the instant the step began, and the hydrograph started at zero, so it concluded nothing was coming. The step then injected the average over those 10 seconds — 333 m³ into a single 25 m² cell, a 13 m column appearing at once. Draining it demanded more water than the cell held, depth went negative, and the safety clamp that zeroes negatives quietly converted that deficit into new water.

Fix. Sample the inflow across the whole step (start, middle, end) and re-check as the step shrinks — which is what the WebGPU kernel already did. Error is now ~1e-15, and a permanent regression test locks it.

The GPU solver was silently dead for weeks — active is a reserved word in WGSL

A tiled-activation optimisation named its per-tile buffer active. That is a reserved keyword in the WebGPU Shading Language, so the entire shader module failed to compile. Nothing crashed: the runtime caught the error and fell back to the CPU, and the interface reported “Processor”, so every run since that change had been quietly using the fallback.

How we caught it. Not by reading the code — by extracting the WGSL from the bundle, compiling it in a real browser through device.createShaderModule() and reading getCompilationInfo(), which answered in one line: “error line 29: ‘active’ is a reserved keyword.” Renaming it to awake compiled clean and the Model tab immediately read “Graphics card (WebGPU)”.

Why it matters twice over. Fixing it turned the GPU path back on — and that is what exposed the second finding: on integrated graphics the GPU path is dramatically slower than the worker. A bug that looked like a pure win led directly to the race described above, which is now what protects the frame rate.

Framer Motion overwrites transform — twice bitten

Two interface bugs had the same root cause: an element set a CSS transform (Tailwind’s -translate-x-1/2, or an inline translate(-50%, -100%)) and animated with Framer Motion, which writes transform itself and threw the positioning away on the first frame. The tour card fell off the bottom of the screen; the dam-picking pill sat half a width off-centre.

A third variant of the same class: the tooltip wrapper used display: contents, which has no box at all, so getBoundingClientRect() returned zeros and every tooltip positioned itself in the top-left corner of the page. It now measures the control inside the wrapper, and falls back to the pointer for labels that are bare text.

Rule for the team: never set a CSS transform on an animated element. Put positioning on a static wrapper, or make it an animated value (x: '-50%') so Motion owns the whole transform.

12. Uncertainty — the ensemble

Twelve runs of the grid solver over a Latin hypercube, varying the three inputs nobody knows before a failure:

  • breach width ×0.6–1.6
  • breach formation time ×0.4–2.5
  • Manning’s n ×0.75–1.35

Ranges reflect the scatter in published breach predictors (Wahl, 2004). Outputs: a chance-of-flooding map layer, a per-place “flooded in X % of runs”, and 10th–90th percentile bands on every headline number.

13. Maps and rendering

  • Terrain: a custom HiResTerrainLayer subclassing deck.gl’s TerrainLayer. Heights come from AWS Terrain Tiles (Terrarium PNG encoding, SRTM-derived, native max zoom 15) bilinearly cut to zoom 17; textures are stitched 2 × 2 from one zoom deeper to reach ~0.5 m per pixel. Smooth normals per mesh (skirts excluded), 16× anisotropic filtering.
  • Tile caching. deck.gl sizes its tile cache from the current viewport, so zooming out and back in discarded what had just been on screen and downloaded it again — which is when a coarse parent tile is left showing as a blurred rectangle among sharp neighbours. The cache is now pinned at 240 tiles regardless of viewport, and a tile that does come back coarse is repaired after 1.2 s (was 4 s) with up to four attempts. Measured over four hard zoom cycles afterwards: 149 tiles loaded, 0 retried, 0 low quality, 120 fps.
  • Aborted requests are normal. Instrumenting three zoom cycles recorded 1,241 imagery responses, all HTTP 200, alongside ~670 net::ERR_ABORTED — those are deck.gl cancelling in-flight tiles as the camera moves, not failures.
  • Imagery: Esri World Imagery to zoom 18, or Esri Dark Gray Canvas plus labels for the map view. Quadrants that cannot be fetched fall back through five coarser zooms before a plain ground colour is used.
  • Aerial perspective: a custom HazeExtension injects distance-based haze into every layer’s fragment shader, matched to the sky colour.
  • The water: the depth raster is painted into an ImageData through a precomputed lookup table (one texture per frame, not one object per cell), drawn on a mesh “skin” lifted 8 m above the terrain and clipped to ground below the crest, with a WaterExtension shader adding three wave trains, a Fresnel sky reflection and a sun glint. Shorelines fade in over the first metre instead of stopping on hard cells.
  • Layer changes cross-fade over 250 ms rather than swapping between frames, and a new scenario fades its layers up the same way.
  • The breach announces itself. When a run starts the camera eases to the dam over 2.2 s and three rings travel out from the breach marker (1.5 s each, 0.36 apart, fading as they widen) — the eye goes to where the water begins before the first frame of flood exists.
  • Performance techniques: the basemap renders at half resolution under 3D terrain; the flood repaints at a fixed ~15 Hz rather than every frame; picking is restricted to the places layer.

14. The interface explains itself

A jury should not need the person who built it standing next to them. Every control and every figure carries its own explanation.

  • Hover explanations everywhere. A Hint component shows a titled explanation after the pointer rests for 450 ms, rendered in a portal so scrolling panels cannot clip it, positioned under the control it describes. Every slider, every segmented option, every scenario-builder field and every headline figure has one — what the setting means, what it changes, and where the number comes from.
  • A guided first run. Four coach marks on a first visit (FirstRun.tsx): describe the failure → run both solvers → follow the water → read what it costs. Each spotlights a real control by a data-coach name, follows it every frame as panels slide, skips any step whose control this window does not show, and is dismissed with Skip or Escape. Seen state lives in localStorage under cascade.tour.v1.
  • Figures count rather than jump. A Count component eases every headline number to its new value over 420 ms. Numbers that snap read as glitches; numbers that travel read as water arriving somewhere.
  • Every figure says where it came from. People exposed, loss of life, flooded area, outflow, critical facilities, bridges, roads cut and indicative loss each explain their method on hover — Graham (1999) rates, AIDR depth × velocity, the JRC curves, the 0.3 m road-cut threshold, OSM-derived populations.
  • The app admits when a run was too short. If the newest arrival time falls within the last two output intervals, the Impact panel says the flood was still reaching new ground when the run ended and points at the Simulated time control. That single line is the difference between a result and a wrong answer.

15. The motion system

All animation comes from one vocabulary in src/lib/motion.ts, so the interface reads as one object rather than a pile of components that each invented a fade. Only transform and opacity are animated — blur and shadow animation on a panel this size costs real frames on an integrated GPU.

TokenValueUsed for
PANEL_SPRINGstiffness 380, damping 31, mass 0.85Panels and sheets — firm, with a touch of overshoot
POP_SPRINGstiffness 520, damping 36Pills, buttons, the run → transport handover
CONTENT_SPRINGstiffness 460, damping 38Sections arriving inside an opening panel
EXIT160 ms, acceleratingEverything leaving — nothing lingers
stagger35 msGap between sections as they land
  • Panels unfold from the edge they live on (origin-left / origin-right) instead of floating in from nowhere, and their sections arrive in reading order. Section declares how it arrives but never starts itself, so it staggers inside an opening panel and sits still anywhere else.
  • Tabs move in the direction you travelled — Event → Model enters from the right, Model → Event from the left.
  • Panel and pill hand over: the pill grows out of the edge the panel collapsed into, 100 ms later, with a hover nudge and a tap press.
  • The top bar and timeline ride in as the loading screen lifts, staggered 40 ms and 140 ms, and the legend rises with the timeline it belongs to.
  • Sheets rise and settle on the house spring with their contents just behind.
  • Run becomes the transport: the big button shrinks away as the play/stop controls grow out of the same spot.
  • MotionConfig reducedMotion="user" is set globally, so a machine configured to reduce motion gets none of it.

Measured during panel animation: 114–120 fps.

16. Comparing runs and models

  • Grid against SPH — the Both view draws them together, the Difference layer maps where they disagree, and the Impact panel scores extent agreement (critical success index), mean depth difference and extent ratio.
  • This run against your last one. “Keep this run to compare” snapshots the current peak-depth envelope as the baseline, with the settings that produced it recorded beside it. Change a slider, run again, and the Difference layer shows what changed, cell by cell, with CSI, hit rate, extent ratio and depth RMSE.
  • Against another model entirely. Import a maximum-depth raster from Delft3D, HEC-RAS or anything else (GeoTIFF or ESRI ASCII, WGS 84 or UTM) and it is scored the same way.
  • Against reality. Import a Sentinel-1 observed extent and get CSI, detection rate and false-alarm ratio against the model.

18. Population and impact — the chain

  1. Population per place comes from the OpenStreetMap population tag where it exists. Where it does not, typical values by place type are used — city 100,000, town 15,000, suburb 5,000, village 1,000, hamlet 150 — and the interface marks those as estimated.
  2. A settlement is not a point. Each has a footprint radius by type: city 1,500 m, town 700 m, suburb 450 m, village 250 m, hamlet 120 m.
  3. People exposed = population × the flooded share of that footprint, sampled every frame — so a half-covered village counts half its people, and the figure grows as the flood spreads.
  4. Hazard class from AIDR Guideline 7-3 (2017), classes H1–H6, combining depth, velocity and depth × velocity. H1 is ≤ 0.3 m with DV ≤ 0.3 (“generally safe”); H6 is above 4 m or DV > 4 (“unsafe for all; all building types vulnerable to failure”).
  5. Loss of life from Graham (1999) fatality rates, chosen by flood severity and warning time. High severity requires DV ≥ 12 (the RESCDAM building-collapse threshold) and arrival under 15 minutes. The panel shows what warning at breach, 30 minutes early and 1 hour early would change — and the brief states how many lives the half-hour buys.
  6. Economic loss from the JRC global depth–damage curve for Asian residential buildings (Huizinga, de Moel & Szewczyk, 2017) — 0.5 m = 33 % of value, 1 m = 49 %, 2 m = 72 %, 6 m = total — against indicative replacement values (₹10 lakh per household at 4.8 people, hospital ₹25 crore, school ₹2 crore, bridge ₹20 crore). Roads count as cut at ≥ 0.3 m depth.

The grid solver appeared not to reach Rishikesh while SPH did, which looked like a bug. It was not. Measured on the standard 100 m grid, 6 h simulated:

23. Known limitations — state these before a judge finds them

  • DEM resolution. SRTM at 30 m, resampled to 100 m cells, captured February 2000. Features narrower than a cell — river channels, embankments, levees, bridges, culverts — are not resolved.
  • No river bathymetry. No satellite DEM sees the channel bed below the water surface, so that storage volume is missing and the valley fills somewhat faster than reality.
  • SRTM is a surface model. It reflects off treetops and rooftops, so in forested valleys the “ground” can read 10–20 m too high.
  • Roughness is a single value for the whole domain unless a land-cover field is supplied, adjustable in the Model tab.
  • The cloudburst is uniform in space. Real storms have cells and move; Cascade rains at one rate over the whole basin. Infiltration is a single constant rate, not a Horton curve or an SCS curve number, and there is no subsurface flow.
  • SPH cannot be rained on. For a cloudburst it receives the storm as lumped runoff entering the head of the reach, so the two solvers are answering slightly different questions and their agreement means less for that event type.
  • A cloudburst wets the whole domain, which defeats the wet-span optimisation, so those runs are inherently heavier than a breach.
  • The GPU path is only used when it wins the race, which on most laptops it will not. That is a property of integrated graphics and of a solver whose per-step cost is dominated by fixed overhead, not a claim that GPUs are slow.
  • Population is OpenStreetMap-derived, with typical values where tags are missing — those are flagged as estimated in the interface.
  • Loss of life and economic loss are indicative, from published rate tables, not a loss adjuster’s assessment.
  • Dam and reservoir figures are approximate public values; they should be verified with the dam owner before any operational use.

24. Q&A cheat sheet

“Did you benchmark this?”

Six analytical and published benchmarks run live in the app — Ritter, grid convergence, Stoker, Environment Agency test 3, dry-bed mass and lake-at-rest — plus mass balance on every run. They caught a genuine mass-conservation bug in our own solver, which we fixed and locked with a regression test.

“Can it do flash floods, or only dam breaks?”

Both. The Cloudburst event rains on every cell of the domain at a rate and duration you set, removes what the ground takes, and routes the rest downhill with the same equations. Nothing has to fail for a flood to happen — which is how most Indian flash-flood deaths actually occur.

“Why is it running on the processor and not the graphics card?”

Because we measured, and on this machine the processor is faster. Both solvers are raced over the first 30 simulated seconds at the start of every run; the browser gave WebGPU the integrated Intel chip, which came back 86× slower, so the worker runs it. On a machine with a discrete GPU the card wins and is used. The Model tab states the verdict in words.

“Why not Malpasset?”

It requires the CADAM 1959 topography — a digitised 1931 survey map — because the valley was reshaped by the flood itself. Modern SRTM would invalidate the comparison, and the dataset is not openly redistributable. We use the reproducible Environment Agency case instead.

“That peak discharge looks impossibly large.”

The large figure is the free-outflow bound, labelled as a bound. The headline number is the modelled peak, routed through the breach against live tailwater, and we show the Froehlich (1995) and MacDonald & Langridge-Monopolis regressions beside it for comparison.

“Why two solvers — isn’t SPH a gimmick?”

They fail differently. SPH carries the flood on moving particles with no fixed grid, so its wet/dry front is not smeared across a cell. Agreement means the answer does not depend on method choice; disagreement is a measurable uncertainty, which the Difference layer maps. We also state SPH’s known bias: it spreads about twice as wide, so an earlier arrival there is not proof the grid solver is late.

“The flood stops before the town downstream.”

Check the run length first — the Impact panel says explicitly when a run ended with water still reaching new ground, and the fix is the Simulated time slider. On Tehri the wave reaches Devprayag at 1 h 35 and Byasi at 4 h 26; six hours simply was not long enough to reach Rishikesh, so the default is now nine.

“Your DEM is too coarse for the plains.”

Agreed, and the app says so in the Model tab. The scenario builder already accepts an uploaded GeoTIFF or ASCII DEM — CartoDEM from Bhuvan, FABDEM or Copernicus — so a better surface can be loaded without changing anything else.

“Does it work for any river?”

Yes. The scenario builder takes any dam or blockage in India by coordinates or from a catalogue of 144 dams across 30 states, fetches terrain and exposure from open data automatically, finds the river direction from the DEM, aligns the dam to its real crest from OpenStreetMap, and for a river blockage measures the impounded lake from the terrain rather than asking for a volume nobody knows.

“How do you know how many people are affected?”

Population from OpenStreetMap tags, with typical values by place type where tags are missing (flagged as estimated). Each settlement is assessed over a footprint sized by type, so exposure is population × the flooded share of that footprint, not a yes/no on a point.

“Can I compare it against our own model?”

Yes — import a maximum-depth raster from Delft3D, HEC-RAS or anything else (GeoTIFF or ESRI ASCII, WGS 84 or UTM) and it is scored cell by cell: critical success index, hit rate, extent ratio and depth RMSE. The same machinery compares one Cascade run against another after you change a setting.
